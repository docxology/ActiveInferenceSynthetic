Active Inference -- A Synthetic Overview

An applicable introduction across equilibrium and far-from-equilibrium systems

1 Abstract

Active Inference casts perception and action as free-energy minimization. This overview shows how Bayesian mechanics carries the Free Energy Principle from equilibrium to far-from-equilibrium life.

2 Extended Abstract

A thing that persists must, in effect, hold a model of the world that keeps it intact. Active Inference turns that observation into mechanics: a system maintains itself by minimizing variational free energy, a single quantity that scores how badly its internal expectations fit incoming sensations [friston2010fep; parr2022active]. Perception lowers free energy by updating beliefs; action lowers it by changing the world so the beliefs come true. One currency, two directions — inference and control — which is why the same template reappears from single cells to brains to institutions.

The bound is what makes this applicable rather than metaphorical. Variational free energy is an upper bound on surprise, the negative log evidence \(-\ln p(s)\) a system assigns to its own sensory states. Because the bound is computable from a system’s own beliefs, minimizing it is approximate Bayesian inference carried out by the physical dynamics of the thing itself. The accounting is organized by a Markov blanket: a statistical boundary splitting external states \(\eta\) from internal states \(\mu\), coupled only through sensory and active blanket states. Internal states come to parameterize beliefs about the external world they can never touch directly — the core claim of Bayesian mechanics, a physics written in terms of, and by, those beliefs [dacosta2021bayesian; ramstead2023bayesian].

fig. 1 shows the move that this overview is built around, without a single equation of derivation. Both panels live on the same free-energy landscape — a Gaussian bowl whose floor is the system’s expected state. At equilibrium (panel a) the flow is pure descent, \(f=-\nabla F\): every trajectory falls straight to the mode and stops. Detailed balance holds, there is no net current, and — taken literally — nothing further happens. A rock is at equilibrium; so, eventually, is a system that has stopped doing anything. Living systems occupy panel (b). There the flow gains a solenoidal term, \(f=(Q-\Gamma)\nabla F\), that runs along the contours of \(F\) rather than down them. Free energy is still minimized — the system still tracks its niche — yet probability mass circulates indefinitely in a non-equilibrium steady state. That persistent current is the signature of things that stay far from equilibrium: heartbeats, foraging, perception that never settles. The gradient part is the Free Energy Principle’s “climbing down surprise”; the solenoidal part is what keeps the system alive while doing so [friston2023bayesian].

Read the two panels together and the headline of Active Inference is visible at a glance: the same imperative — minimize free energy — produces inert relaxation in one regime and open-ended, self-sustaining behavior in the other. The rest of this overview unpacks that picture in the spirit of show, not tell: each subsequent section adds one runnable, minimal demonstration rather than another layer of formalism.
